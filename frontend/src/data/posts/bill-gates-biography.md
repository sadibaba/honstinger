Few people have shaped the modern technology industry as much as Bill Gates. He co-founded Microsoft, helped bring personal computers into offices and homes, and later turned his attention to global health, education, and climate change. His career can be read as two stories: one about building a software company at the start of the personal computer era, and another about trying to apply money, data, and technology to large public problems. Both stories are shaped by the same traits: curiosity, competitiveness, and a habit of studying a problem in great detail.

## Early life and a head start with computers

William Henry Gates III was born on October 28, 1955, in Seattle, Washington. He grew up in a family that valued reading, education, and public service. His father was a lawyer, and his mother was active in community and charitable organizations. As a child he was known as a quick learner who enjoyed books, puzzles, and competitive games.

A major turning point came at Lakeside School, a private school in Seattle. In the late 1960s, Lakeside gave students access to a computer terminal, which was rare at the time. Gates became fascinated with programming and spent many hours learning how software worked. There he met Paul Allen, a slightly older student who shared his interest. The two worked on programs together, and the friendship became the foundation for their future business.

### Harvard and the decision to leave

Gates enrolled at Harvard University in 1973. He was a strong student in mathematics, but he spent much of his time in the computer lab. In 1975, Allen showed him a magazine article about the Altair 8800, an early microcomputer sold as a kit. They realized that the new machine would need software, and that a programming language could make it far more useful.

Gates and Allen developed a version of the BASIC language for the Altair. The project worked well enough to launch a business, and Gates left Harvard to focus on it. Microsoft was founded in 1975, initially as a small company selling programming tools for early personal computers.

## Building Microsoft

The company's early strategy was simple but powerful: write software that could run on many different computers rather than tie it to one manufacturer. In the early 1980s, Microsoft agreed to provide an operating system for IBM's new personal computer. Microsoft acquired and adapted an existing system, which became MS-DOS. Because many other companies built computers compatible with the IBM design, DOS spread widely, and Microsoft became central to the PC market.

Gates served as chief executive and was deeply involved in product planning, technical decisions, and business strategy. Under his leadership, Microsoft developed Windows, which brought a graphical interface to a very large number of computers, and Microsoft Office, which became a standard set of tools for documents, spreadsheets, and presentations. The company grew from a small team into one of the most valuable businesses in the world, and Gates became one of the best-known entrepreneurs of his generation.

### Competition and scrutiny

Microsoft's success also brought criticism. Regulators and competitors argued that the company used its position in operating systems to disadvantage rivals, and it faced major antitrust cases in the United States and elsewhere. These cases influenced how people thought about platform power in the technology industry, a debate that continues today with cloud services, app stores, and artificial intelligence. Gates and Microsoft defended their business practices, but the period showed that a dominant technology company can attract close legal and public attention.

## Stepping back from Microsoft

Gates stepped down as chief executive in 2000, and Steve Ballmer took the role. Gates remained involved for several years as chief software architect, then reduced his daily work at Microsoft in 2008 so that he could spend more time on philanthropy. He later served as chairman of the board and left the board in 2020. By then, Microsoft had moved far beyond its original desktop software business into cloud computing, subscriptions, and developer tools.

## Philanthropy and global health

In 2000, Bill and Melinda Gates created the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, which became one of the largest private charitable organizations in the world. Its work has focused on global health, poverty, education, and agriculture. The foundation has supported vaccination programs, efforts to reduce diseases such as polio and malaria, and research into improving nutrition and farming in lower-income countries.

Gates also helped start the Giving Pledge in 2010 together with Warren Buffett, encouraging wealthy people to commit large portions of their fortunes to philanthropy. In 2025, the foundation announced that it plans to spend most of its resources over the following two decades and then close. Supporters see this approach as a focused attempt to solve specific problems. Critics sometimes raise questions about how much influence one wealthy donor or foundation should have over public policy and global priorities.

## Climate, writing, and a public voice

In the 2010s, Gates became increasingly interested in energy and climate change. He supported research into cleaner energy technologies and helped launch Breakthrough Energy, an initiative that invests in new climate-related ventures. He explained his views in the book *How to Avoid a Climate Disaster*, and he has written other books about technology, business, and public health, including *The Road Ahead* and *How to Prevent the Next Pandemic*. He also writes publicly about books, science, and development through his own channels.

His opinions attract strong reactions. Many readers value his data-driven style and long-term view, while others disagree with specific recommendations or question whether technology alone can solve social problems. That mix of admiration and debate is part of his public role.

## Legacy

Bill Gates's legacy is unusually broad. In technology, he helped establish software as a major industry and helped make computers a normal part of daily work. In philanthropy, he has used his wealth to support public health and development on a very large scale. His story also shows how the same person can be praised for innovation and questioned over market power, influence, and priorities.

What remains clear is that his career began with a simple idea: if computers became widely available, software would matter enormously. That idea changed how people work, learn, and communicate, and it continues to shape the world far beyond the company he started.
