If there were a world championship for failure, Jack Ma would have won it before he ever won at business. He failed his exams. He was rejected from 30 jobs. Even KFC — a fried chicken restaurant — refused to hire him. Harvard turned him down ten times.

And yet, this man built Alibaba, one of the largest companies on Earth, and became China's richest man. His story is proof that the world's biggest losers can become its biggest winners.

## The Boy Who Talked to Tourists

Ma Yun — later known to the world as Jack Ma — was born on September 10, 1964, in Hangzhou, China, into a poor family. His parents were traditional musicians and storytellers. Money was scarce. China at that time was closed to the outside world.

But young Jack had one obsession: learning English.

At age 12, he woke up early every morning and rode his bicycle to the Hangzhou Hotel — the one place in the city where foreign tourists stayed. For nine years, he gave free tours to tourists, rain or shine, not for money, but to practice English. They corrected his grammar and told him stories about the outside world.

One tourist friend, Ken Morley from Australia, became so close that he later sent Jack money to help him through college, and even invited him to Australia in 1985 — Jack's first trip outside China. He later said that trip opened his mind more than a decade of study.

A pen pal saved my life. Without those free tours, there would be no Alibaba.

The tourists even gave him the name "Jack" — because his real name, Ma Yun, was hard for foreigners to pronounce.

## The Champion of Failure

Jack Ma's academic record is one of the most hilarious failure stories in business history:

- Primary school: Failed twice
- Middle school: Failed again
- College entrance exam: Failed three times. The first attempt, he scored 1 out of 120 in mathematics. One point.
- Harvard: Applied and was rejected ten times
- Jobs: Applied to 30 jobs and was rejected by every single one

The most famous rejection came at KFC. When the fried chicken chain opened in Hangzhou, 24 people applied for the job. 23 were hired. The only one rejected was Jack Ma.

He also applied to be a police officer — 4 applied, 3 were accepted, and he was the only one cut. He applied to Harvard for the MBA program and was rejected again. Even when he became a teacher, his salary was around $12–15 a month.

I failed so many times. I went for a job with KFC — 24 people applied, 23 got hired. I was the only one not hired.

Years later, these rejections became his armor. When the internet business crushed him again and again, nothing surprised him anymore. Failure was his normal.

## 1995: The Discovery That Changed China

In 1995, Jack Ma — now an English teacher — traveled to the United States to help translate a business deal. The trip went badly (he was even reportedly held at gunpoint in a dispute), but in Seattle, a friend showed him something magical: the Internet.

Jack typed his first search: "beer." Results showed German beer, American beer, Japanese beer — but no Chinese beer. He searched "China" — almost nothing came back.

His mind exploded. China — a nation of over a billion people — was completely invisible on the internet. He quit his teaching job that year and returned home with a borrowed computer and a vision.

He started China Yellow Pages, one of China's first internet companies. Everyone called him crazy — friends said he was a con man selling something that didn't exist. At that time, the internet barely worked in China, and many officials didn't even know what a computer was. The company failed. He then joined a government-run e-commerce firm — which also went nowhere.

## The Apartment Speech: 18 People, $60,000, One Dream

In 1999, at age 35, Jack Ma gathered 17 friends and students in his small apartment in Hangzhou. No fancy office. No investors. Just a sofa, a television playing a news report about the internet, and a man with a dream.

He gave a legendary speech — recorded on video — that has become business history:

We are going to be a great company... If our competitors are strong, we will be stronger. Alibaba will change the lives of billions of people... Our competitors are in Silicon Valley and Europe. But China is ours.

He asked them to pool their savings. Together they scraped together about $60,000. The site launched with one clear mission:

Let there be no difficult business in the world.

Alibaba.com — a marketplace connecting Chinese manufacturers with the world — was born.

## Six Minutes That Raised $20 Million

Investors rejected Alibaba again and again — he collected over 30 rejections in Silicon Valley, including from investors who laughed him out of their offices. Even his own early investor team doubted him.

Then came one of the most famous meetings in venture capital history. Masayoshi Son, the legendary founder of Japan's SoftBank, gave Jack Ma exactly six minutes to pitch. Ma spoke about his vision, his mission, and his failures.

Son interrupted: You keep the same look as the people I admire. Before the pitch time was even over, Son decided: SoftBank would invest $20 million — later followed by even more. That investment grew into one of the most profitable in history, worth tens of billions.

Goldman Sachs led an earlier $5 million round. But it was Son's belief that saved Alibaba — as China's tech world crashed during the dot-com bust of 2000, Alibaba survived because of that money.

## David vs. Goliath: How Taobao Ate eBay

In 2002-2003, the American giant eBay — the world's biggest online marketplace — entered China and bought out its local competitor. Everyone said Alibaba was finished. eBay had billions; Jack Ma had a startup.

So Jack quietly created Taobao — a free consumer marketplace — and launched it in secret from a team that worked in a hidden apartment. While eBay charged listing fees, Taobao was free for sellers. While eBay ignored local needs, Taobao added chat features so buyers and sellers could talk like humans.

The business world laughed at the size mismatch. Three years later, eBay closed its China operations in defeat. Jack Ma — the man rejected by KFC — had defeated one of Silicon Valley's giants on his home soil.

Alibaba then launched Alipay (its own payment system), which became China's answer to PayPal — and later Ant Group, one of the world's largest financial platforms.

## The $25 Billion IPO — The Biggest in History

On September 19, 2014, Alibaba listed on the New York Stock Exchange. The offering raised $25 billion — the largest IPO in world history at that time. When Jack Ma rang the opening bell, the shy English teacher who once earned $12 a month was suddenly worth over $20 billion and was named the richest man in China.

But unlike most CEOs, Ma never presented himself as a tech genius. He couldn't code. He once joked:

I'm not a tech guy. I'm looking at the internet with a beginner's mind. That's my advantage.

His genius was vision, storytelling, and people. He famously told Alibaba employees:

We are not a technology company. We are a dream company.

He set an unusual goal: Alibaba must last 102 years — spanning three centuries (1999 to 2101). His culture code stressed values over rules, customers over shareholders, and "employees second, shareholders third."

## The Fall: The Speech That Silenced the King

In 2019, at age 55 — on Chinese Teachers' Day (September 10, his birthday) — Jack Ma stepped down as Alibaba's chairman, saying he wanted to return to his first love: education. It was a rare voluntary exit at the top — most billionaires never leave their thrones.

Then came the moment that changed everything.

In October 2020, at a financial summit in Shanghai, Ma delivered a fiery speech criticizing Chinese regulators — calling them outdated and mocking the banking system. Days later, the Chinese government suspended the $37 billion IPO of Ant Group — which would have been the largest in history — just two days before listing.

Then, Jack Ma essentially vanished from public view. For over three months, he made no public appearances. The world's media ran headlines: Where is Jack Ma? His empire's stock lost over $100 billion in value. Regulators reshaped his businesses. The man who was China's loudest businessman became its quietest.

He eventually reappeared — quieter, rarely speaking publicly, seen traveling, painting, and teaching. His fortune fell, but his influence on the world's business history remains permanent.

## The Comeback of Spirit: Philanthropy and Teaching

Even before his fall, Ma had been giving back. The Jack Ma Foundation focuses on rural education, entrepreneurship, and women's development — nearly half of Alibaba's early founders and partners were women, something Ma proudly called his "secret weapon."

During COVID-19, his foundations shipped millions of masks and test kits across the world — including to the US, Africa, and Europe.

His favorite identity remained simple:

My dream was to be a teacher. If I had not built Alibaba, I would have stayed an English teacher — and I would have been happy. Teachers change lives.

## Lessons from Jack Ma's Life

1. Failure is the best university — He failed at everything, and that's exactly why nothing could stop him later.
2. Rejection is protection — Every "no" redirected him toward a bigger "yes."
3. Learn constantly — Nine years of free tours gave him English, and English gave him the world.
4. See what others can't — In 1995, the whole of China was invisible online; he saw a billion-dollar gap.
5. Heart beats money — Investors followed his belief, not his business plan.
6. Think in decades, act today — "Today is hard, tomorrow is harder, but the day after tomorrow is beautiful."
7. Stay humble — The richest man in China still called himself a teacher first.

## Conclusion

Jack Ma's story is the ultimate underdog epic. A skinny kid from a poor family who failed math, lost every job interview, and couldn't even get hired at KFC — went on to build a company that touches over a billion people daily.

He himself summarized it best:

Never give up. Today is hard, tomorrow will be worse, but the day after tomorrow will be sunshine. If you give up tomorrow, you will never see the sunlight.

He was rejected 30 times — and each rejection carved one more piece of the legend. The message for every dreamer is simple: the world doesn't remember how many times you fell; it remembers how big you stood back up.
